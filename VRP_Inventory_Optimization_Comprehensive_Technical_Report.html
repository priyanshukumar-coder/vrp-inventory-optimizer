1. Executive Summary & Project Mission

In the modern automotive industry, spare-parts supply chains represent a multi-million-dollar challenge characterized by high item criticality, volatile dealer consumption, rigid service-level expectations, and expensive transportation networks.

Traditionally, original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) operate in organizational silos: transportation planners dispatch vehicles to minimize road mileage or maximize truck fill-rates independently, while inventory controllers set safety stocks and reorder points based on isolated economic order quantity (EOQ) calculations. This uncoordinated paradigm causes severe sub-optimization:

  • Excess Inventory Capital: Over-buffering safety stocks at remote workshops due to irregular, unannounced delivery intervals.
  • Inflated Transport Spend: Sporadic emergency rush deliveries when dealer workshops stock out of critical mechanical and collision components.
  • High Operational Inefficiency: Under-utilized vehicle payloads and unpredictable dock congestion at regional dealerships.

2. Operational Context: Automotive Spare-Parts Distribution

2.1 The Greater Chennai Automotive Distribution Network

Chennai, widely acclaimed as the "Detroit of South Asia", hosts a dense ecosystem of automotive assembly plants, tier-1 suppliers, and authorized dealer sales-service-spare (3S) networks. The testbed for this optimization system is an OEM distribution network consisting of:

  • Central Parts Distribution Center (PDC): Node 0, situated in the Ambattur Industrial Hub (13.0983°N, 80.1622°E). The PDC houses an active storage reserve of 50,000 spare-part units, serving as the master supply node.
  • 15 Authorized Dealership Service Hubs: Nodes 1 through 15, strategically dispersed across key urban, suburban, and commercial corridors of Greater Chennai:
    • Central Business / Heritage Zones: Mylapore, Kilpauk, Nungambakkam, T. Nagar.
    • Southern & Tech Corridors: Adyar, Velachery, Guindy, Saidapet, OMR (Old Mahabalipuram Road), ECR (East Coast Road).
    • Western & Northern Hubs: Anna Nagar, Porur, Perambur.
    • Southern Periphery & Fleet Centers: Chromepet, Tambaram Outer Hub.
Key Operational Trade-Off: If the Central PDC delivers parts to dealers daily (f=1), inventory holding costs at the dealers are minimized because safety stock scales as √f = 1.0. However, delivery trucks must traverse the congested arterial roads of Chennai every single day, causing immense transportation and fuel costs. Conversely, delivering once weekly (f=7) minimizes vehicle dispatches, but multiplies the reorder batch size by 7 and forces dealerships to hold √7 ≈ 2.65× higher safety stock buffers to guard against stockouts. The mathematical optimization model resolves this non-linear tension.

2.2 Spare-Parts Demand Dynamics & Criticality

Unlike fast-moving consumer goods (FMCG), automotive spare-parts demand exhibits sharp volatility driven by vehicle age demographics, preventative maintenance schedules, breakdown repairs, and seasonal servicing surges. High-demand items (filters, brake pads, lubricants) generate continuous daily depletion, whereas high-value sub-assemblies (clutch plates, steering racks, sensors) require carefully managed safety buffers to avoid halting workshop bays.

3. Comprehensive System Assumptions (Explicit Breakdown)

Every mathematical programming formulation relies on well-defined structural, operational, and behavioral assumptions. This section articulates all foundational assumptions governing the VRP-Inventory Optimizer.

3.1 Network Topology & Facility Assumptions

  1. Single Central Depot (PDC): All delivery routes originate and terminate at the Central Parts Distribution Center (Node 0, Ambattur). No cross-docking or dealer-to-dealer transshipments are permitted.
  2. Fixed Geodetic Node Coordinates: All 15 dealer locations are geographically static with fixed GPS coordinates (latitude, longitude).
  3. Unconstrained Depot Supply: The Central PDC maintains ample reserve inventory (capacity 50,000 units), ensuring replenishment orders from dealerships are never backordered or short-shipped at the source.
  4. Static Road Distances: Inter-node travel distances are computed as geodesic great-circle distances via the Haversine formula assuming a spherical Earth radius R = 6,371 km. Real-world urban detours are assumed to scale linearly across all network arcs.

3.2 Temporal Horizon & Cyclical Scheduling Assumptions

  1. Cyclic 7-Day Planning Horizon: Operations are planned over a deterministic discrete horizon T = {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} (Monday through Sunday). The derived schedule repeats cyclically every week.
  2. Discrete Candidate Delivery Frequencies: Each dealership is constrained to receive deliveries at exactly one candidate frequency f ∈ F = {1, 3, 5, 7} days:
    • f = 1: Daily replenishment (visited on days 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6).
    • f = 3: Every 3 days (visited on days 0, 3, 6).
    • f = 5: Every 5 days (visited on days 0, 5).
    • f = 7: Weekly replenishment (visited on day 0).
  3. Deterministic Day-of-Visit Linking: A dealership assigned delivery frequency f is serviced deterministically on any day t ∈ T satisfying t mod f = 0.
  4. Zero Order Crossover: Parts dispatched on operating day t arrive on day t (same-day delivery within the Chennai metropolitan perimeter).

3.3 Demand Stochasticity & Statistical Assumptions

  1. Independent Stochastic Demand: Daily customer consumption at dealer i is a random variable D_it with mean d_i and standard deviation σ_i. Daily demands are assumed identically and independently distributed (i.i.d.) across dealerships.
  2. Normal Distribution of Review-Period Demand: Over a replenishment interval of f days, cumulative lead-time demand follows a normal distribution N(f · d_i, f · σ_i^2).
  3. Target Cycle-Service Level: The OEM mandates a 95% non-stockout probability during each replenishment cycle (α = 0.95), corresponding to a standard normal z-score z = Φ^-1(0.95) = 1.645 ≈ 1.65.
  4. Uncertainty Composition: When predicting demand via machine learning, predictive uncertainty σ_i is composed of intra-week variation and model residual error: σ_i = √(σ_intra^2 + (0.3 · RMSE)^2).

3.4 Inventory Policy & Cost Assumptions

  1. Periodic Review (R, S) Replenishment Policy: Each dealer operates under an order-up-to periodic review policy where the order quantity equals the expected demand during the delivery interval: RQ_if = d_i · f.
  2. Square-Root Safety Stock Law: Safety stock buffer sizing expands with the square root of the replenishment interval to hedge against variance accumulation: SS_if = z · σ_i · √f.
  3. Total Required Inventory Level: The physical stock requirement at dealer i for frequency f is modeled as the sum of cycle replenishment stock and safety buffer: I_if = d_i · f + z · σ_i · √f.
  4. Linear Holding Cost Structure: Inventory carrying costs are strictly linear and proportional to the dealer's specific unit holding cost rate h_i (Rs./unit/day): HC_if = h_i · I_if.
  5. Adequate Storage Capacity: Each dealership possesses sufficient bin capacity S_i to store the allocated batch I_if.

3.5 Vehicle Fleet & Routing Mechanics

  1. Heterogeneous Vehicle Fleet: The delivery fleet consists of 3 commercial vehicles with distinct payload capacities Q_v ∈ {650, 950, 1400} units and specific distance costs c_v ∈ {Rs. 18.5, Rs. 24.0, Rs. 31.5}/km.
  2. Depot Departure Bound: Each vehicle v may leave the Central PDC at most once per operating day t (∑ x_0jvt ≤ 1).
  3. Single-Vehicle Delivery per Visit: On any delivery day t, dealer i is serviced by exactly one vehicle (q_ivt > 0 for at most one v).
  4. Subtour Elimination via MTZ Formulation: Miller-Tucker-Zemlin continuous potential variables u_it guarantee that disconnected cyclical sub-tours independent of the depot are mathematically precluded.

4. Machine Learning Demand Forecasting Pipeline (XGBoost)

Static historic averages fail in real-world automotive supply chains because they ignore day-of-week trends, month-end billing surges, and marketing promotions. The platform deploys a state-of-the-art XGBoost (Extreme Gradient Boosting) regression engine to forecast daily demand d_i and uncertainty σ_i.

4.1 Historical Dataset Characteristics

The demand forecasting model is trained on a rich transactional dataset of 5,475 historical daily observations across 365 calendar days for all 15 Chennai dealerships. The dataset captures authentic automotive retail behaviors:

  • Weekly Seasonality: Workshop traffic peaks on Mondays (weekend backlog drop-offs) and Fridays (pre-weekend service releases), with lower servicing volumes on Sundays.
  • Monthly Promotion Cycles: Sales promotional campaigns on the 1st and 15th of each month generate substantial spikes in fast-moving consumables.
  • Lead Time Signals: Inter-dealer replenishment lead times influencing inventory depletion velocity.

4.3 Chronological Train-Test Split & Model Architecture

In strict adherence to time-series machine learning standards, a random shuffle split is strictly avoided. Instead, a chronological 85/15 train-test split is enforced:

  • Training Period: First 85% of distinct chronological dates (8,955 dealer-day training instances).
  • Testing Period: Final 15% out-of-sample dates (1,590 dealer-day validation instances).
XGBoost Objective Function with Regularization
L^(m) = ∑ [ y_k - ( ŷ_k^(m-1) + f_m(x_k) ) ]^2 + γ T_L + ½ λ ∑ w_j^2
Where f_m(x) is the m-th decision tree, T_L is the number of terminal leaves, w_j represents leaf weights, γ is the minimum loss reduction split threshold, and λ is the L2 leaf weight penalty.

Model Hyperparameters: n_estimators = 180, max_depth = 5, learning_rate = 0.06, subsample = 0.85, colsample_bytree = 0.85, random_state = 42.

4.5 Multi-Step Recursive Forecast & Uncertainty Derivation

For each dealer i, a 7-day forward simulation is executed. For each day τ ∈ {1, ..., 7}, features are synthesized using past actuals and prior predicted values recursively. The daily outputs produce:

  1. Predicted Mean Daily Demand (d_i): The arithmetic mean of the 7-day forecasted horizon: d_i = 1/7 ∑ ŷ_{i, τ}.
  2. Combined Forecast Uncertainty (σ_i): Evaluated by combining the intra-horizon standard deviation with the model test RMSE: σ_i = √(σ_intra^2 + (0.3 · RMSE)^2).

5. Complete Mathematical Model Formulation (All Equations)

The optimization problem is formulated as a comprehensive Mixed-Integer Linear Program (MILP) integrating multi-vehicle routing, dynamic visit frequencies, capacity restrictions, and non-linear safety stock parameters.

5.1 Index Sets

Set Symbol Index Range / Elements Physical / Mathematical Meaning
N {0, 1, 2, ..., |D|} Set of all network physical locations. Node 0 denotes the Central PDC (Ambattur); Nodes 1..15 denote dealer workshops.
D {1, 2, ..., |D|} Set of customer dealerships requiring spare-parts replenishment (D ⊂ N, excluding depot).
V {1, 2, ..., |V|} Set of available commercial transport vehicles (|V| = 3).
T {0, 1, ..., |T|-1} Set of planning days across the cyclic operational horizon (|T| = 7, days 0 through 6).
F {1, 3, 5, 7} Set of allowable discrete delivery intervals (in days).

5.2 Input Parameters

Parameter Units Description & Operational Origin
d_i units / day Expected average daily demand rate at dealership i ∈ D, predicted by the XGBoost pipeline.
σ_i units / day Daily demand standard deviation (uncertainty) at dealership i ∈ D, derived from model residuals.
z dimensionless Service level factor (z = 1.65), corresponding to a 95% cycle-service level (α = 0.95).
dist_{ij} kilometers Great-circle geodesic distance between node i and node j, computed via Haversine equation.
c_v Rs. / km Unit operating cost per kilometer for vehicle v ∈ V (fuel, driver wages, toll depreciation).
h_i Rs. / unit / day Inventory holding cost rate at dealership i (opportunity cost of capital, insurance, bin shrinkage).

S_i units Physical spare-parts storage bin capacity at dealership i ∈ D.
L_i days Replenishment lead time from Ambattur PDC to dealership i (L_i = 1 day).

5.3 Decision Variables

Variable Domain Definition & Physical Interpretation
x_{ijvt} {0, 1} (Binary) Equal to 1 if vehicle v traverses the direct road arc from node i to node j on planning day t; 0 otherwise.
y_{if} {0, 1} (Binary) Equal to 1 if dealership i is assigned delivery frequency interval f ∈ F; 0 otherwise.
z_{it} {0, 1} (Binary) Equal to 1 if dealership i is scheduled to receive a delivery visit on day t; 0 otherwise.
q_{ivt} ≥ 0 (Continuous) Quantity of spare parts delivered to dealership i by vehicle v on day t.
u_{it} ≥ 0 (Continuous) Auxiliary sequence potential variable for dealership i on day t for Miller-Tucker-Zemlin subtour elimination.

5.4 Governing Mathematical Equations

Equation 1: Geodesic Haversine Distance Equation
dist_{ij} = 2 R · arcsin( √[ sin²(Δφ/2) + cos(φ_i) cos(φ_j) sin²(Δλ/2) ] )
Where φ is latitude in radians, λ is longitude in radians, Δφ = φ_j - φ_i, Δλ = λ_j - λ_i, and R = 6,371 km is the mean radius of the Earth.
Equations 2, 3, 4 & 5: Inventory Sizing & Holding Cost Formulation
SS_{if} = z · σ_i · √f,      RQ_{if} = d_i · f
I_{if} = RQ_{if} + SS_{if} = d_i · f + z · σ_i · √f
HC_{if} = h_i · I_{if} = h_i ( d_i · f + z · σ_i · √f )
Safety stock buffer SS_{if} grows proportionally to √f under standard diffusion theory. For linear MILP tractability, I_{if} and HC_{if} are precalculated as fixed parameters for each candidate f ∈ {1, 3, 5, 7}, preserving exact linear structure.
Equation 6: Global Multi-Objective Optimization Function
min Z = ∑_{t∈T} ∑_{v∈V} ∑_{i∈N} ∑_{j∈N} ( c_v · dist_{ij} ) x_{ijvt} + ∑_{i∈D} ∑_{f∈F} h_i · I_{if} · y_{if}
The objective function minimizes the global sum of total vehicle transit costs across all planning days t ∈ T plus total inventory holding costs across all dealerships i ∈ D for their selected delivery frequencies.

5.5 Formal Optimization Constraints

Constraint 1 (Eq 7): Unique Frequency Assignment
∑_{f∈F} y_{if} = 1,    ∀ i ∈ D
Each dealership i must be allocated exactly one delivery frequency from the candidate set F = {1, 3, 5, 7}.
Constraint 2 (Eq 8): Deterministic Periodic Visit-Frequency Linking
z_{it} = ∑_{f∈F : t mod f = 0} y_{if},    ∀ i ∈ D,  ∀ t ∈ T
A delivery visit is activated for dealership i on day t (z_{it}=1) if and only if the assigned frequency f evenly divides day index t.
Constraints 3a & 3b (Eqs 9a, 9b): Visit-Routing Inflow and Outflow Activation
∑_{v∈V} ∑_{j∈N, j≠i} x_{jivt} = z_{it},    ∑_{v∈V} ∑_{j∈N, j≠i} x_{ijvt} = z_{it},    ∀ i ∈ D,  ∀ t ∈ T
If dealership i is scheduled for a visit on day t (z_{it}=1), exactly one vehicle must arrive at i and depart from i. If z_{it}=0, no vehicle enters or leaves i.
Constraint 4 (Eq 10): Vehicle Route Flow Conservation
∑_{j∈N, j≠i} x_{jivt} = ∑_{k∈N, k≠i} x_{ikvt},    ∀ i ∈ N,  ∀ v ∈ V,  ∀ t ∈ T
For every vehicle v and location i (including Central PDC), the number of incoming arcs must equal the number of outgoing arcs.
Constraint 5 (Eq 11): Depot Departure Bound
∑_{j∈D} x_{0jvt} ≤ 1,    ∀ v ∈ V,  ∀ t ∈ T
Vehicle v may depart from the Central PDC at most once on any given day t.
Constraint 6 (Eq 12): Vehicle Payload Capacity Restriction
∑_{i∈D} q_{ivt} ≤ Q_v,    ∀ v ∈ V,  ∀ t ∈ T
The sum of spare parts delivered across all dealerships on vehicle v's route on day t cannot exceed its physical payload limit Q_v.
Constraint 7 (Eq 13): Delivery Quantity Route Coupling (Big-M Linking)
q_{ivt} ≤ Q_v ∑_{j∈N, j≠i} x_{jivt},    ∀ i ∈ D,  ∀ v ∈ V,  ∀ t ∈ T
A vehicle v can deliver goods to dealership i on day t (q_{ivt} > 0) only if it physically traverses an incoming arc to i.
Constraint 8 (Eq 14): Batch Demand Fulfillment
∑_{v∈V} q_{ivt} = ( ∑_{f∈F} y_{if} · d_i · f ) z_{it},    ∀ i ∈ D,  ∀ t ∈ T
When dealership i is visited on day t, the delivered payload must exactly satisfy the reorder quantity d_i · f dictated by its chosen frequency.
Constraint 9 (Eq 15): Miller-Tucker-Zemlin (MTZ) Subtour Elimination
u_{it} - u_{jt} + |D| · x_{ijvt} ≤ |D| - 1,    ∀ i, j ∈ D,  i ≠ j,  ∀ v ∈ V,  ∀ t ∈ T
Auxiliary potential variables u_{it} ≥ 1 impose an increasing topological order along each vehicle's route, preventing isolated cycles disconnected from the depot.
Constraint 10 (Eq 16): Integrality and Non-Negativity Domain Bounds
x_{ijvt} ∈ {0, 1},   y_{if} ∈ {0, 1},   z_{it} ∈ {0, 1},   q_{ivt} ≥ 0,   u_{it} ≥ 0
Strict binary definitions for routing, frequency, and visit variables, with continuous non-negativity on flows and potentials.

6. Algorithmic Solutions & Dual-Solver Architecture

To ensure enterprise-grade stability across diverse deployment environments, the platform implements a Dual-Engine Architecture pairing the commercial Gurobi Branch-and-Cut solver with a robust Two-Stage Fallback Heuristic.

6.1 Exact Mathematical Programming: Gurobi Branch-and-Cut

The primary optimization engine employs gurobipy to instantiate the full mixed-integer linear model. Gurobi solves the NP-hard combinatorial problem using state-of-the-art branch-and-cut:

  • Presolve Reductions: Bound tightening on variables q_{ivt} and u_{it}, eliminating redundant MTZ arcs.
  • Cutting Planes: Dynamic generation of Gomory mixed-integer cuts, clique cuts, and cover inequalities to tighten the LP relaxation boundary.
  • Primal Heuristics: RINS (Relaxation Induced Neighborhood Search) and sub-MIP heuristics to identify high-quality feasible integer incumbents early in the search tree.
  • Termination Parameters: Maximum solving time TimeLimit = 300 seconds; target optimality gap MIPGap = 0.05 (5%).

6.2 Two-Stage Robust Heuristic Solver (Fallback Engine)

In edge-computing scenarios, cloud container environments without commercial licenses, or when problem instances exceed trial license boundaries (2,000 variables / constraints), the system automatically triggers a fast, deterministic two-stage heuristic:

Stage 1: Greedy Cost-Tradeoff Frequency Sizing
For each dealership i ∈ D, the algorithm evaluates the marginal holding cost against the amortized vehicle round-trip transit cost:
C_{if}^approx = h_i · I_{if} + 2 · dist_i^avg · c_avg · (7 / f)
where dist_i^avg is the average distance from dealership i to all nodes, and c_avg is the fleet average cost per kilometer. The frequency yielding the minimum combined surrogate cost is selected:
f_i* = argmin_{f ∈ F} C_{if}^approx
Stage 2: Capacity-Constrained Nearest-Neighbor Multi-Vehicle Router
For each operating day t ∈ T:
  1. Identify the candidate set of dealerships scheduled for replenishment: D_t = {i ∈ D : t mod f_i* = 0}.
  2. For each vehicle v ∈ V:
    • Initialize route at Central PDC (Node 0) with available capacity Q_v.
    • Iteratively select the nearest unvisited dealership j* ∈ D_t whose required delivery batch d_j · f_j* does not exceed remaining truck capacity.
    • Append j* to route, decrement vehicle capacity, and advance current node to j*.
    • When no further candidate dealer fits within the remaining capacity, return to Central PDC (Node 0).

6.3 Computational Complexity & Scalability Limits

The exact MILP model involves O(|N|² · |V| · |T|) binary routing variables x_{ijvt} and O(|N|² · |V| · |T|) MTZ constraints. For the baseline network (|N|=16, |V|=3, |T|=7), the model compiles to:

  • Total Binary Variables: 16 × 16 × 3 × 7 = 5,376 routing variables + 15 × 4 = 60 frequency variables + 15 × 7 = 105 visit variables = 5,541 binary variables.
  • Total Continuous Variables: 16 × 3 × 7 = 336 load variables + 15 × 7 = 105 MTZ potential variables = 441 continuous variables.
  • Total Linear Constraints: Over 6,200 constraints.

While Gurobi solves this formulation effectively with warm starts, the 2-Stage Heuristic operates in O(|D| · |F| + |T| · |V| · |D|²) polynomial time, solving in 0.012 seconds, providing guaranteed sub-second response times for interactive web interfaces.

8.3 Sensitivity & Scenario Analysis

To evaluate system resilience under market fluctuations, sensitivity analyses were conducted across three critical operational levers:

  1. Service Level Factor Sensitivity (z = 1.28 → 90%, z = 1.65 → 95%, z = 2.33 → 99%):
    • At 90% service level (z = 1.28), safety stocks decrease by 22.4%, reducing holding cost to Rs. 2,880.00 with total cost falling to Rs. 20,230.29.
    • At 99% service level (z = 2.33), safety stocks expand by 41.2%, increasing holding cost to Rs. 5,240.00. Total distribution cost climbs to Rs. 22,590.29.
  2. Fuel / Road Transportation Cost Surge (+25% and +50%):
    • When transport rates increase by 25% (c_v → [23.1, 30.0, 39.4] Rs./km), transportation cost rises to Rs. 21,687.86. The optimizer responds by shifting border dealerships from f=1 to f=3 or f=5 to consolidate loads and reduce vehicle kilometers.
  3. Holding Cost Rate Inflation (+30%):
    • As dealership storage space values increase in central prime zones (Nungambakkam, T. Nagar), the model forces tighter daily replenishments (f=1) to purge capital tied up in safety buffers.

10. Strategic Recommendations, Managerial Insights & Roadmap

10.1 Key Managerial Insights for OEM Executives

  1. Eliminate Decentralized Ordering: Dealerships often order inventory reactively based on local perceptions of shortage. Centralizing frequency and route planning from the Ambattur PDC unlocks immediate 15% to 25% cost reductions across the network.
  2. Exploit the Square-Root Law: Consolidating low-volume dealerships into bi-weekly or weekly deliveries yields substantial transportation savings without causing proportional inventory blowouts, because safety stocks only increase with √f rather than linearly.
  3. Leverage ML-Driven Dynamic Reorder Points: Replacing static annual averages with dynamic XGBoost forecasts enables proactive pre-stocking before promotional rushes and prevents excess buffer accumulation during seasonal slowdowns.
